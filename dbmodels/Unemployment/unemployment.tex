\section{Overview}

The existence of involuntary unemployment has long been recognized as one the
main ills of modern industrialized economies, making old Keynesian economics
relevant in analyzing and fixing unemployment untill the
70s.\ \cite{keynes1937general} argued that unemployment was the result of weak
aggregate demand: radical uncertainty about future states of the economy leads
entrepreneurs and households to hold precautionary saving, making aggregate
consumption and investment too weak for the economy to reach full employment.

At the end of the 60s, \cite{phelps1967phillips}, Milton Friedman (among
others) questioned way the Phillips curve was designed, and criticized
particularly the absence of expectations in the formation process of prices
and inflation.\ Oil price shocks in the 70s proved how wrong were some
assumptions of the keynesian model, in particular the price rigidity and the
absence of expectations. \cite{lucas1976econometric} contributed to the
building a new framework were involuntary unemloyment was absent. Despite the
central role of unemployment in the policy debate, that variable has been
absent from this new generation of models that have become the workhorse for
the analysis of monetary policy, referred to as New Keynesian.

Meantime, one strand of the economic analyzed unemployment as search and
matching process between households and firms.\ Early contributions to the
current vintage of search and matching models include
\cite{diamond1982aggregate}, \cite{diamond1982wage},
\cite{mortensen1982matching} \cite{pissarides1984search}.\ These models offers
a central role to the interactions between firms and households in terms of
job matching and wage negotiating. However, these contributions were partial
equilibrium, and were not yet embedded into a dynamic macroeconomic model.
These literature is referred to as the Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides models.

Since the 2000, economists started to include the DMP\ model into real
business cycles models and new keynesian models as \cite{shimer2005cyclical}
and \cite{hall2005employment}. This note aims at presenting the analytics of
such model with a low set of frictions.

Since then, there have been a large number of estimated version of the labor
matching model.\ See \cite{gertler2009unemployment},
\cite{gali2012unemployment} and \cite{christiano2016unemployment}.

\section{The model}

\subsection{Households}

Suppose a welfare maximizing household enjoying current and future consumption
and dislike working with respective curvatures $\sigma_{C}$, $\sigma_{H}$
$>0$.\ The $j$-th household maximizes the following stream of utilities
discounted at a fixed rate $\beta$:%
\begin{equation}
\max_{\left\{  c_{jt},b_{jt},n_{jt}\right\}  }\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}%
\beta^{\tau}E_{t}\left\{  \frac{\varepsilon_{t+\tau}^{C}c_{jt+\tau}%
^{1-\sigma_{C}}}{1-\sigma_{C}}-\chi n_{jt+\tau}\right\}
\end{equation}
where $c_{jt}$\ is consumption, $n_{jt}$ is the fraction of employed workers
within the family j, and $\chi>0$\ is a shift parameter allowing to pin down
the amount the employment rate in the deterministic steady state. The AR(1)
shock $\varepsilon_{t}^{C}$\ captures exogenous changes in the utility
obtained from consumption. Assuming that the size of the working age
population is one, the number of family number is also one,, so the shared
unemploymed within the family is given by:%
\begin{equation}
n_{jt}=1-u_{jt}%
\end{equation}
The law of motion of employment is given by:%

\begin{equation}
n_{jt}=\left(  1-\rho\right)  n_{jt-1}+x_{t}u_{t-1}%
\end{equation}
where $\rho\in\lbrack0,1]$ is the separation rate with the term $\rho
n_{jt-1}$ that can be interpreted as the frictional unemployment.\ The term
$x_{t}u_{t-1}$\ is the number of unemployed workers who have found a job
between t-1 and t, while $(1-x_{t})u_{jt-1}$\ is the fraction who stays
unemployed between $t-1$ and $t$. Here, the hiring rate $x_{t}$\ is decided by
firms, not by the household this is why there is no $j$ subscript. Similarly,
household $j$ is so small that its own employment decisions has no implication
on the unemployemnt rate $u_{t-1}$.

The budget constraint in real terms reads as:%
\begin{equation}
c_{jt}+i_{jt}+f\left(  \Delta i_{jt}\right)  i_{jt-1}+b_{jt}=r_{t-1}%
b_{jt-1}+n_{jt}w_{t}+z_{t}k_{jt-1}+\left(  1-n_{jt}\right)  \bar{b}-T_{jt},
\end{equation}
where $b_{jt}$ is the amount of real bonds, $r_{t-1}$ is the pre-determined
real interest rate of real government bonds $b_{jt-1}$, $w_{t}$ the real wage
is computed as $w_{t}=W_{t}/P_{t}$ and a lump-sum tax financing the government
$T_{jt}$. Here, the fraction of the family in employment is remunerated by the
real wage $w_{t}$\ while the fraction of unemployed $1-n_{jt}$ obtains an
unemployment insurance $\bar{b}$. Note that $i_{jt}$ is the flow of investment
subject to adjustment costs with $f\left(  x\right)  =0.5\kappa\left(
x-1\right)  ^{2}$ and $\Delta i_{jt}=i_{jt}/i_{jt-1}$. Physical capital reads
as follows:%
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{t}^{i}i_{jt}=k_{jt}-\left(  1-\delta\right)  k_{jt-1}.
\end{equation}


Because the number of households is one, we drop the subscript j.\ The
Lagrangian problem reads as:%
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}_{t} &  =\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\beta^{\tau}\left[  \varepsilon
_{t+\tau}^{C}\frac{C_{t+\tau}^{1-\sigma_{C}}}{1-\sigma_{C}}-\chi n_{t+\tau
}\right]  \\
&  +\beta^{\tau}\lambda_{t+\tau}^{C}\left[  r_{t-1+\tau}b_{t-1+\tau}%
+n_{t+\tau}w_{t+\tau}+\left(  1-n_{t+\tau}\right)  \bar{b}+z_{t+\tau
}k_{t-1+\tau}-c_{t+\tau}-f\left(  \Delta i_{t+\tau}\right)  i_{t-1+\tau
}-i_{t+\tau}-b_{t+\tau}-T_{t+\tau}\right]  \\
&  +\beta^{\tau}\lambda_{t+\tau}^{C}V_{t+\tau}^{H}\left[  \left(
1-\rho\right)  n_{t-1+\tau}+x_{t+\tau}u_{t-1+\tau}-n_{t+\tau}\right]  \\
&  +\beta^{\tau}\lambda_{t+\tau}^{C}q_{t+\tau}\left[  k_{t+\tau}-\left(
1-\delta\right)  k_{t-1+\tau}-\varepsilon_{t+\tau}^{i}i_{t+\tau}\right]
\end{align*}
where $\lambda_{t}^{C}$, $q_{t}$ and $V_{t}^{H}$\ are lagrangian multipliers
on each constraints. Note that both\ $V_{t}^{H}$ and $q_{t}$\ are
pre-multiplied by the marginal utility of consumption to express unemployment
in terms of current utility-equivalent consumption.

First order conditions are given by:%
\begin{align}
c_{t} &  :\varepsilon_{t}^{C}c_{t}^{-\sigma_{C}}-\lambda_{t}^{C}=0\\
b_{t} &  :\beta E_{t}\left\{  \lambda_{t+1}^{C}\right\}  r_{t}-\lambda_{t}%
^{C}=0\\
n_{t} &  :-\chi+\lambda_{t}^{C}\left(  w_{t}-\bar{b}\right)  -\lambda_{t}%
^{C}V_{t}^{H}+\beta\lambda_{t+1}^{C}\left(  1-\rho\right)  V_{t+1}^{H}=0\\
i_{t} &  :-\lambda_{t}^{C}\left(  1+f^{\prime}\left(  \Delta i_{t}\right)
\right)  -\lambda_{t}^{C}q_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{i}-\beta\lambda_{t+1}%
^{C}f^{\prime}\left(  \Delta i_{t+1}\right)  =0\\
k_{t} &  :\beta\lambda_{t+1}^{C}z_{t+1}+\lambda_{t}^{C}q_{t}-\beta
\lambda_{t+1}^{C}q_{t+1}\left(  1-\delta\right)  =0
\end{align}


\subsection{Firms}

Firms technology is given by the following production function%
\begin{equation}
y_{it}=\varepsilon_{t}^{A}k_{it-1}^{\alpha}n_{it}^{1-\alpha},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{t}^{A}$ is the a technology shock, $k_{t-1}$\ is the
physical stock of capital with fixed intensity of $\alpha\in\lbrack0;1]$ and
$n_{t}$ is the employment demand.

As for households, the law of motion of employment:%
\begin{equation}
n_{it}=\left(  1-\rho\right)  n_{it-1}+x_{it}u_{t},
\end{equation}
where $x_{it}$\ is the hiring rate of the i-th firm that premultiply the stock
of unemployed.

Profits reads as:%
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{t}=y_{it}-w_{t}n_{it}-z_{t}k_{it-1}-G\left(  x_{it}\right)  u_{t}%
\end{equation}
where the cost of increasing hirings reads as in \cite{gali2010monetary} and
\cite{blanchard2010labor}:
\[
G\left(  x_{it}\right)  =\frac{\Gamma}{1+\phi}x_{it}^{1+\phi}%
\]
where $\phi$ is the elasticity of hiring costs with respect to the hiring
rate, and $\Gamma$\ is a constant which allows to pin down the total cost of
hiring to one percent of total production as in \cite{blanchard2010labor}.
Variable $x_{it}$ is the probability of hiring a new worker, the associated
costs can be interpreted as monitoring cost paid by firms to find a potential
new worker.

The dynamic Lagrangian problem reads as:%
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}_{t}  &  =E_{t}%
%TCIMACRO{\tsum _{\tau=0}^{\infty}}%
%BeginExpansion
{\textstyle\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}}
%EndExpansion
\beta^{s}\frac{\lambda_{t+\tau}^{C}}{\lambda_{t}^{C}}\{\left[  y_{it+\tau
}-w_{t+\tau}n_{it+\tau}-z_{t+\tau}k_{it-1+\tau}-G\left(  x_{it+\tau}\right)
u_{t+\tau}\right] \\
&  +V_{t+\tau}^{P}\left[  \left(  1-\rho\right)  n_{it-1+\tau}+x_{it+\tau
}u_{t+\tau}-n_{it+\tau}\right]  \}
\end{align*}
Note that $\beta^{s}\lambda_{t+\tau}^{C}/\lambda_{t}^{C}$\ is the stochastic
discount factor.\ As households are shareholders of firms because they own the
physical capital, firms has the same discounting scheme as households. Also
note that the firm takes as given the labor market situation (here measured as
the unemployment rate) $u_{t}$, this is because each firm is so small that it
is assumed that they cannot influence individually the aggregate unemployement rate.%

\begin{align*}
n_{it}  &  :(1-\alpha)\frac{y_{it}}{n_{it}}-w_{it}-V_{t}^{P}+\beta
\frac{\lambda_{t+1}^{C}}{\lambda_{t}^{C}}\left(  1-\rho\right)  V_{t+1}%
^{P}=0\\
x_{it}  &  :-G^{\prime}\left(  x_{it}\right)  u_{t}+V_{t}^{P}u_{t}=0\\
k_{it-1}  &  :\alpha\frac{y_{it}}{k_{it-1}}-z_{t}=0
\end{align*}


The standard hiring decision rate is given by this standard no arbitrage
condition:%
\[
\underset{\text{Marginal cost of hiring}}{\underbrace{G^{\prime}\left(
x_{it}\right)  }}=\underset{\text{marginal profit of hiring}}{\underbrace
{(1-\alpha)\frac{y_{it}}{n_{it}}-w_{it}}}+\underset{\text{Future gains from
keeping the worker employed}}{\underbrace{\beta\frac{\lambda_{t+1}^{C}%
}{\lambda_{t}^{C}}\left(  1-\rho\right)  G^{\prime}\left(  x_{it+1}\right)  }}%
\]


\subsection{Wage bargaining}

Bilateral bargaining between firms and workers. Following form:%
\[
S_{t}=\underset{\text{household marginal utility}}{\underbrace{\left(
V_{t}^{H}\right)  }}^{\eta}\underset{\text{firm marginal profit}}%
{\underbrace{\left(  V_{t}^{P}\right)  }}^{1-\eta}%
\]
where $\eta$\ is the relative bargaining power of households. Period-by-period
Nash bargaining implies that the firm and each of its workers determine the
wage in period t by solving the problem:%
\begin{equation}
\max_{\{w_{t}\}}S_{t}%
\end{equation}
The solution to that problem implies the following constant share rule:%
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial S_{t}}{\partial w_{t}} &  =0\Leftrightarrow\eta\frac{\partial
V_{t}^{H}}{\partial w_{t}}\frac{S_{t}}{V_{t}^{H}}+\left(  1-\eta\right)
\frac{\partial V_{t}^{P}}{\partial w_{t}}\frac{S_{t}}{V_{t}^{P}}=0\\
&  \Leftrightarrow\eta V_{t}^{P}=\left(  1-\eta\right)  V_{t}^{H}%
\end{align*}
In order to take into account some exogenous change in the negotiation power
of households, we augment $\eta$ by an AR(1) shock, denoted $\varepsilon
_{t}^{m}$, as follows:%
\[
\varepsilon_{t}^{m}\eta V_{t}^{P}=\left(  1-\varepsilon_{t}^{m}\eta\right)
V_{t}^{H}%
\]


To illustrate how this equations determine the wage, we can rewrite substitute
$V_{t}^{P}$\ and $V_{t}^{H}$:%
\[
w_{t}=\eta\left[  (1-\alpha)\frac{y_{it}}{n_{it}}+m_{t,t+1}V_{t+1}^{P}\right]
+\left(  1-\eta\right)  \left[  -\chi/\lambda_{t}^{C}-b+m_{t,t+1}V_{t+1}%
^{H}\right]
\]
where $m_{t,t+1}=\beta\lambda_{t+1}^{C}/\lambda_{t}^{C}\left(  1-\rho\right)
$. It is straightforward to see that the wage is the result between the
desired wage for firms and household weighted by the bargaining power of each agent.

\subsection{Authorities}

Public spending $G_{t}$ is exogenous, as it follows an AR(1) shock process
$\varepsilon_{t}^{G}$, which is expressed as a constant portion of output:
\[
G_{t}=g^{y}\bar{Y}\varepsilon_{t}^{G}.
\]
The balance sheet reads as:%
\[
r_{t-1}b_{t-1}+G_{t}+bu_{t}=b_{t}+T_{t},
\]
where $b_{t}$\ are government bonds.

\subsection{Equilibrium conditions}

And total demand is given by:%
\begin{equation}
Y_{t}=C_{t}+I_{t}+G_{t}+G\left(  x_{t}\right)  u_{t}.
\end{equation}
Unemployment rate:%
\begin{equation}
u_{t}=1-n_{t}%
\end{equation}
Note that as in Blanchard \& Gali (2012), it is assumed that hiring costs are
set to match 1\%\ of output in the deterministic steady state such as
$G\left(  \bar{x}\right)  \bar{u}=0.01\bar{Y}.$%
