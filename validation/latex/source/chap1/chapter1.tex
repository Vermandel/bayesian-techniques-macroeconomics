\section{What is a business cycle?}

\subsection{A\ brief history}
Back in the 1930s, the emergence of national accounting allowed economists to
get an accurate measure of aggregate production from the demand, supply, and
income sides. National accounts provide a complete and consistent set of
techniques to measure a country's economic activity. Given these new measures
of economic activity, the study of business cycles quickly emerged. As
explained by \cite{schumpeter1930mitchell}, the pioneer of the concept of
business cycles can be traced back to \cite{mitchell1913business}'s work on the
interpretation of gold, prices, and wages during the US Civil War in his
treatise on \textit{Business Cycles}. He provided the first quantitative
study of business cycles. His pioneering work laid the foundation for modern
business cycle analysis in \cite{burns1946measuring} by defining a business
cycle as \textquotedblleft a \textit{type of fluctuation found in the
aggregate economic activity of nations that organize their work mainly in
business enterprises: a cycle consists of expansions occurring at about the
same time in many economic activities, followed by similarly general
recessions, contractions, and revivals which merge into the expansion phase of
the next cycle; in duration, business cycles vary from more than one year to
ten or twelve years; they are not divisible into shorter cycles of similar
characteristics with amplitudes approximating their own}.\textquotedblright

The contributions of Mitchell established the first dichotomy in economics by
separating long-term and short-term analyses in macroeconomics. In particular,
\cite{burns1946measuring} provided the first definition of the duration of a
business cycle, which lies between one and twelve years. This definition of
the business cycle has evolved over time; modern business cycle analysis
determines the frequency of a business cycle as a fluctuation lying between 6
to 32 quarters, which is shorter than the duration suggested by
\cite{burns1946measuring}. Any component of a macroeconomic time series with a
Components with periods between 6 and 32 quarters are retained as business-cycle fluctuations;
components with periods longer than 32 quarters are assigned to the trend.

A clear typology of an economic cycle is a primary aspect of macroeconomic
analysis, particularly for policymakers. Consider a government facing an
adverse event (financial turmoil, pandemic, war, etc.); dating the recession,
gauging its magnitude and duration are all crucial for determining the size of
any fiscal/monetary stimulus to mitigate the crisis.\footnote{For instance, in
the European Union, there has been ongoing debate since the Maastricht Treaty
about fiscal rules and public deficits. In order to maintain a balanced
government budget, the EU may penalize any member state exhibiting excessive
structural deficits. The critical aspect lies in the definition of the
'structural' nature of the deficit, which typically relies on business cycle
measures. A change in the computation of the cyclical budget deficit may
alter the stance of the EU Commission on national fiscal rules.} During the
last century, economic associations, exemplified by the NBER and CEPR, were
established to provide start and end dates of recessions for the US and
Europe, respectively. Therefore, significant efforts have been made by
economists to clearly typify the economic cycle by disentangling periods of
expansion, crisis, recession, and recovery in macroeconomic time series.

Prior to the 1940s, much of the economic cycle analysis focused on determining
the periodicity of cycles. You may have heard during your undergraduate studies
about the Juglar, Kuznets, and Kondratiev cycles. These cycle analyses aim to
explain how long an innovation, investment, or other spending may create a
long-lasting cycle (i.e., determining the duration of an expansion cycle
followed by a recession). Following the contributions of
\cite{burns1946measuring}, cycle analysis shifted towards disentangling time
series into cyclical versus trend components. This change in cycle analysis
also resulted in some theoretical interpretations of the cycle through the
establishment of short-run and long-run macroeconomics (fluctuations vs.
growth). These materialize through new theories of economic growth (e.g.,
\cite{harrod1948towards}, \cite{solow1956contribution}) and theories of
fluctuations (e.g., \cite{keynes2018general}, \cite{samuelson1948foundations}).
In this lecture, we will focus solely on the business cycle theory which
emerged in the 1970s and 1980s.

\subsection{Measuring business cycles}

However, the frontier separating short run and long run analysis is however
not as clear as what one would expect. The emergence of econometrics in the
second half of the 20$^{th}$ century had also been accompanied by new
statistical methods that aim at separating low and high components in economic
time series. Any macroeconomist working in a time series face the following
problem:%
\begin{equation}
y_{t}=\tau_{t}+\psi_{t},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{t}$ is the secular and $\psi_{t}$ the cyclical component of time
series $y_{t}$.\ How can we compute $\tau_{t}$ and $\psi_{t}$? Unfortunately,
there is no unique method to disentangle $\tau_{t}$ and $\psi_{t}$. Therefore,
the measure of business cycles is still an open question.\ In this section, we
will discuss three different methods to extract the business cycle component
from the data.

A first preliminary step prior working on real data is to apply logs.\ Why do
we use logs? Because GDP data exhibits an exponential shape. To illustrate
this aspect, let us assume for now that economic growth is following an
exponential growth process (a very common assumption).\ Formally, Let $Y_{t}$
denote the GDP that follows the following data-generating process:
\begin{equation}
Y_{t}=Y_{0}\left(  1+\gamma\right)  ^{t}\exp(\varepsilon_{t}). \label{eq bs}%
\end{equation}
In this expression, $\left(  1+\gamma\right)  ^{t}$\ is the (exponential)
trend, $\varepsilon_{t}$\ is the fluctuation term and $Y_{0}$\ is the initial
observation of output. In this expression, the interpretation of $\gamma$\ is
simply the quarterly percentage growth rate. Applying logs to this expression,
we 'linearize' the expression (\ref{eq bs}) by flattening the exponential
shape of output:%
\begin{align}
\log(Y_{t})  &  =\log(Y_{0})+\log(1+\gamma)t+\varepsilon_{t}\\
&  \simeq\underset{\tau_{t}}{\underbrace{y_{0}+\gamma\cdot t}}+\underset
{\psi_{t}}{\underbrace{\varepsilon_{t}}}.
\end{align}
As the final expression is linear, it is possible to estimate intercept
$y_{0}$ and slope $\gamma$ through OLS.\ From these estimated parameters, the
trend can be computed and removed from level output to obtain what we usually
interpret as the fluctuation term $\psi_{t}$ in GDP time serie. See first
MATLAB box to see alternative filtering methods to extract $\psi_{t}$\ from
$Y_{t}$.%

We apply three filtering methods on the log of GDP to illustrate some
alternative measures of business cycles:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textsf{A linear trend:} In a nutshell, we use OLS regression to
estimate the slope of growth. This method assumes that the data-generating
process is the same as in \autoref{eq bs}. It allows for the quick extraction
of the business cycle component without losing important information in the
data. However, if output permanently shifts following a structural break,
then the measure of the business cycle may be biased. For example, following
the Great Recession in 2007, the US never returned to the pre-crisis trend,
resulting in a permanently negative business cycle component after 2007.
Considering the potential for structural changes in economic growth, it may
be useful to consider a time-varying trend.

\item \textsf{The HP filter:} The statistical method of
\cite{hodrick1997postwar} extracts the cyclical component of a time series by
separating long-term and short-term fluctuations. This method is two-sided,
meaning that for computation in period \( t \), it requires both past and
future values. Thus, end-of-sample estimations are typically biased. In
addition, the filter may omit information in the data that could be relevant
for analysis. This method has faced many critiques, summarized by
\cite{hamilton2018you}.

\item \textsf{The band-pass filter:} A band-pass filter is a device that
passes frequencies within a certain range and attenuates frequencies outside
that range. In economics, the band-pass filter can be used to isolate the
component of a time series within a particular frequency band to separate the
business cycle, the medium-term cycle, and the long-term cycle (i.e., the
trend). This is useful, for instance, to examine if an economic relationship
(e.g., money-inflation) remains significant across different frequencies of
the cycle. \cite{baxter1999measuring} and \cite{christiano2003band} provide
the two most renowned applications of the band-pass filter in business cycle
analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The analysis of business cycles}

\cite{blanchard1986business} provides a nice description of modern business
cycle analysis:\ \textquotedblleft\textit{Fluctuations in economic activity
are seen as the result of small, white noise shocks that affect the economy
through a complex dynamic propagation system.}\textquotedblright\ Pioneered by
\cite{frisch1933propagation}, theoretical interpretations of economic
fluctuations (i.e.\ the term $\varepsilon_{t}$ in \autoref{eq bs}) rely on
system response analysis following the realization of some shocks. The
dominant view is that even large fluctuations result from small
shocks.\footnote{Some more recents applications to financial turmoils, such
as\ \cite{kiyotaki1997credit} or \cite{boissay2016booms}, show that small
shock can trigger large depressions.} Particular economic fluctuations can be
ascribed to particularly large shocks followed by periods during which the
economy returns to equilibrium. This interpretation contrasts with the
\textquotedblleft old\textquotedblright\ Keynesian approach of economic
fluctuations: the latter interpret fluctuations as imbalances in money or
labor markets.

According to \cite{lucas1977understanding}, the business cycle exhibits some
regularities stable over time:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The main regularity is at a sectoral level: output movement across
sector are strongly serially correlated.\ So if one sector experiences one
boom, it's very likely that all other sector experience also experience a boom too.

\item Production of durable goods is more volatile than non-durable goods.

\item Price are procyclical.

\item Short term interest rates are procyclical.

\item Monetary aggregates and velocity measures are procyclical.
\end{enumerate}

Given these observations, \cite{lucas1977understanding} argued that
\textquotedblleft\textit{all business cycle are alike}\textquotedblright. This
assertion has important implications for theorists, as it implies that these
business cycle regularities can be all interpreted from a single general
theory of fluctuations, the real business cycle theory. In the following
years, Lucas has provided several critical contributions to the foundation of
a theory of business cycles. The main ingredients are a general equilibrium
with infinitely-living and representative agents that form their expectations
rationally. This handout provides the basis of business cycle theory pioneered
by Bob Lucas.

\section{An illustration of a business cycle model through the
consumption-saving problem}

According to \cite{lucas1977understanding}, a theory of real business cycle
aims at providing any point in time de value of output.\ In particular, it
provides some theoretical interpretations of the empirical relations across
macroeconomic time series, based on agent-level decisions (usually referred to
as \textit{microfoundations}).\ In this section, we present the
consumption-saving problem to examine how changes in\ (expected) consumption
affect\ the equilibrium real rate.

\subsection{A toy model of consumption-saving}

Consider an\ simplified version of the \cite{lucas1978asset}'s asset pricing
model.\ This model is characterized by discrete-time and an infinite horizon
economy populated by \textit{households}, which are infinitely lived and of
measure one. This is an endowment economy, so the production is assumed to be
fully exogenous and driven by an exogenous shock:%
\begin{equation}
y_{t}=1-\rho+\rho y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\text{ with }\varepsilon_{t}%
\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}), \label{eq: exo cons}%
\end{equation}
The production in this economy stems from the presence of infinitively-lived
trees, output is the fruit that falls from trees. Therefore, we interpret
$\varepsilon_{t}$\ as the weather that disrupt the quantity of fruits that the
tree can provide (i.e., for a low realization of $\varepsilon_{t}$, the tree
provides a lower quantity of fruits). The weather exhibits some persistence,
so a low realization of the weather today is likely to affect the quantity of
fruits in the next period by a degree $\rho$. To illustrate this persistence,
a storm may damage a branch, the latter requires few periods to heal and
produce fruit as usual. In this model, the only source of uncertainty is about
current and future values of $\varepsilon_{t}$, as the household ignore how
much of fruits she will have.\ This uncertainty can be addressed by the
purchased of some financial claims that we discuss hereafter.

Regarding the preferences, each household maximizes her lifetime utility,
measured as the current and future discounted streams of utility:%
\begin{equation}
\max_{\left\{  c_{t},b_{t}\right\}  }\mathbb{E}_{t}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}%
\beta^{s}u\left(  c_{t+s}\right)  , \label{fig: utility}%
\end{equation}
where $\beta$\ is the discount factor.\footnote{For $\beta=1$, the household
is perfectly patient and does not discount the future, \textit{i.e.} consuming
now or later provides exactly the same utility ($\beta^{0}u\left(  c\right)
=\beta^{1}u\left(  c\right)  =\beta^{2}u\left(  c\right)  $). For any value of
$\beta<1$, the household is impatient, the lower $\beta$ is, the more
impatient the household is ($\beta^{0}\mathcal{U}\left(  c\right)  >\beta
^{1}\mathcal{U}\left(  c\right)  >\beta^{2}\mathcal{U}\left(  c\right)  $).
Microeconomic evidence suggests that households are impatient, so the discount
factor should lie between 0 and 1 (usually close to one).} We impose a
logarithm for the utility function $u\left(  c_{t}\right)  =\log(c_{t})$.
Expression $\mathbb{E}_{t}$\ denotes the expectation operator.\ In a rational
expectation equilibrium, agents can form expectations about the future states
of the economy at no cost.\ However in a stochastic environment, the
expectation of $\mathbb{E}_{t}\left\{  X_{t+1}\right\}  $ is assessed with all
information available in $t$ to agents, meaning that any shock that will
occurs between $t$ and $t+1$ will not be anticipated: a gap between the
expectation of $\mathbb{E}_{t}\left\{  X_{t+1}\right\}  $ and its realization
$X_{t+1}$ is observed, thus creating a source of fluctuations.

Regarding the financial aspect of the model,\ we depart from
\cite{lucas1978asset} to introduce a (risk free) financial claim in the model.
Given the uncertainty stemming from the weather, the household may be
interested in purchasing a financial claim to hedge against low realizations
of the weather.\ Put differently,\ the household may choose to consume all the
fruits today, or postpone consumption to the next period by depositing the
fruits to a bank.\ The bank is perfectly competitive, and provide (fruit)
deposit services and provide an interest rate $r_{t}$ for each unit of fruit
deposited between $t$ and $t+1$. The fruit is the numeraire, so the interest
rate $r_{t}$\ is directly interpreted in units of fruits.

So each period, the households has to possibility to consume $c_{t}$\ units of
fruits or deposit $b_{t}$ to the bank. The budget constraint faced by the
household at any point in time is given by:%
\begin{equation}
c_{t}+b_{t}\leq b_{t-1}r_{t-1}+y_{t}, \label{eq:budget}%
\end{equation}
where the left-hand side of the equation gathers the spending of the
household, while the right-hand side gather the income from deposits and the
production from trees. Note that when the household takes it deposit
decisions, she knows the interest rate, $r_{t-1}$, so there is no uncertainty
on the return the household may get from depositing fruits in $t+1$.

The Lagrangian problem solved by the household is given by:%
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\beta^{s}\left[  \log\left(  c_{t+s}%
\right)  \underset{}{-}\lambda_{t+s}\left(  c_{t+s}+b_{t+s}-b_{t-1+s}%
r_{t-1+s}-y_{t+s}\right)  \right]  , \label{eq:lagrangian}%
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{t}$\ denotes the Lagrangian multiplier over the budget
constraint. Two important assumptions are implicitly set on this Lagrangian
problem:\ (\textit{i}) the budget constraint is also discounted, so the
constraint is eternally binding over time (i.e., it binds today and in the
future); (\textit{ii}) the budget constraint binds to equality, so the income
is fully spent in \autoref{eq:budget}.\footnote{Note that we can check whether
the constraint always binds to equality by tracking the non-negativity of the
Lagrangian multiplier.}

Maximizing \autoref{eq:lagrangian} in $c_{t}$\ and $b_{t}$ yield the following
first order conditions:%
\begin{align}
(c_{t})  &  :\lambda_{t}=\frac{1}{c_{t}}\label{eq: FOC1}\\
(b_{t})  &  :\lambda_{t}=\beta E_{t}\left\{  \lambda_{t+1}\right\}  r_{t}
\label{eq: FOC2}%
\end{align}
Note that if the household consumes and saves by adopting these two equations,
she will get the highest feasible level of lifetime utility over the business
cycles. We provide an interpretation of these two equations in the following subsection.

\subsection{A discussion of the first order conditions}

The two first order conditions (\ref{eq: FOC1}-\ref{eq: FOC2}) are one of the
main ingredient of real business cycle theory: the \textsf{Euler equation}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textsf{Marginal utility of consumption (MUC)}.\ 
\end{itemize}

In equilibrium, the Lagrangian multiplier over the budget constraint is equal
to the marginal utility of consumption: $\lambda_{t}=u^{\prime}(c_{t})$.\ The
functional form of the utility function $u(c_{t})$ critically matters to
explain the saving-consumption nexus.\ So this section provides some insights
about the interpretation of the utility function. \begin{figure}[h]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chap1/tikz/MUC.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Consumption, utility and marginal utility cycles under logarithmic
utility}%
\label{fig MUC}%
\end{figure}

Consider an exogenous consumption cycle as defined in \autoref{eq: exo cons},
what are the implications of this cycle in terms of utility function
$u(c_{t})$ and marginal utility of consumption $\lambda_{t}$ for the
household? \autoref{fig MUC} reports some illustrative simulations of
consumption, and their corresponding streams of utility and marginal utility.

When consumption is high in period 3, the individual utility also increases
with the level of consumption, so there is a perfect co-movement between
utility and consumption. In contrast, the marginal utility exhibits a
different pattern of fluctuations with respect to consumption.\ When
consumption is high, the willingness to consume is low.\ Why?\ Because of
\textbf{diminishing marginal utility}, the consumption of each additional unit
of a good gives less extra utility than the consumption of the previous unit.
Think about a cyclist running the Tour de France who is thirsty following some
physical effort to run up a hill. At first, he's really willing to drink water
($\lambda_{t}$ is high), so the first glass of water provides a lot of
utility.\ For the second glass, he becomes less thirsty so the consumption of
the second glass does provide utility but not as much as the previous
one.\ This feature of the marginal utility explains the negative relation
between consumption and the willingness to consume.\ When consumption is low,
the utility from consuming an additional unit of goods is higher than when
consumption is high.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textsf{Intertemporal\ Marginal Rate of Substitution (IMRS).}
\end{itemize}

The second core key ingredient of modern business cycle theory is the Euler
equation. Consider optimal condition on bonds:
\begin{equation}
u^{\prime}(c_{t})=\beta E_{t}\left\{  u^{\prime}(c_{t+1})\right\}  r_{t}.
\label{eq FOC3}%
\end{equation}
The left hand side of this equation provides the utility loss of saving today
(priced in terms of present marginal utility), and the right hand side is the
utility gain at the next period. As long as this equation holds to equality,
there is a no-arbitrage condition that makes the household equally well off
between consuming today or tomorrow. Note that the equilibrium interest rate
plays here a key role as it directly determines how households are weighting
consumption today versus tomorrow: if the equilibrium rate rises, households
are more likely to postpone consumption and to consume more at the next
period. Therefore, the key ingredient here is the pricing scheme that affects
the trade-off between consuming now and tomorrow.\ The intertemporal marginal
rate of substitution of future for present consumption is given by $u^{\prime
}(c_{t})/E_{t}\left\{  u^{\prime}(c_{t+1})\right\}  $.

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth]{chap1/tikz/TMS.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Optimization scheme for the representative household}%
\label{fig TMS}%
\end{figure}

The intertemporal analysis of consumption is actually exactly the same as in
the standard consumer problem describing the maximization of the utility by
determining the optimal quantity of two types of goods under a budget
constraint.\ The difference here is that instead of having two different
goods, the trade-off now applies between consuming today and tomorrow.\ The
budget constraint becomes intertemporal and shapes the dynamics of the
interest rate.

\autoref{fig TMS} reports the joint-determination of the optimal level of
financial assets and consumption.\ In the left panel, we show the consumption
possibilities ($c_{t},c_{t+1}$) which are offered by financial markets in the
economy (and implicitly here by the production capacities of the economy which
are exogenous).\ The slope of this curve,$-\beta\mathbb{E}_{t}\left\{
r_{t+1}\right\}  $, is decreasing because current and future consumption are substitutes.

In the right panel of \autoref{fig TMS}, we introduce the household's
preference with respect to consumption.\ These preferences materialize in a
graphical representation as indifference curves. These curves are different
pairs ($c_{t},c_{t+1}$) that provides exactly the same utility so that an
individual is equally well of in any point of the indifference curve. Given
the real rate on deposits, the household determines the levels of present and
future consumption ($c_{t}^{\ast},c_{t+1}^{\ast}$) maximizing her utility
given the consumption possibilities offered by financial markets. In
equilibrium A, the household find the unique possible path of consumption
maximizing her utility intertemporally and is called \textit{the consumption
point}. \autoref{eq FOC3} is actually the (unique) consumption point that
provides the highest level of lifetime utility for a given budget constraint.

\subsection{General Equilibrium}

Finally, we need to set one condition over deposits to close the model. In
financial autarky (\textit{i.e.}, with no trading partners), the general
equilibrium is jointly determined by the equilibrium on financial and goods
markets, as the optimal condition of saving determine the amount of
consumption. So clearing one market also clears the others.

In absence of external borrowing/lending, at a macroeconomic level, the
following condition on domestic saving emerges:%
\begin{equation}
b_{t}=0. \label{eq general equilibrium}%
\end{equation}
Note that since households populating our economy are all symmetric, the agent
specific saving is also zero in equilibrium.\ Given this relation, the budget
constraint in \autoref{eq:budget} becomes:%
\begin{equation}
c_{t}=y_{t}.
\end{equation}


Therefore, the model can be casted as the following set of two equations:%
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
y_{t}=1-\rho+\rho y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\\
r_{t}=\beta^{-1}E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}  /y_{t}%
\end{array}
\right.  . \label{eq:sys1}%
\end{equation}
The first equation is the quantity of fruit falling from trees, and the second
equation is the equilibrium condition on deposits. In absence of endogenous
production, the number of fruit over time determines the equilibrium interest
rate that ensure that \autoref{eq general equilibrium} holds to equality.

Note that in this simple setup, the household does not optimally choose
consumption, because it is exogenous (falling from trees).\ As a result, the
consumption-saving is not really a saving problem because saving is zero in
equilibrium. So the interpretation of the model is the following: the agent in
$t$ contemplates how much fruits $y_{t}$ she has by observing $\varepsilon
_{t}$ (i.e.\ the weather). Given this new information, the agent plans how
much fruits $y_{t+1}$ he may expect at the next period. Given the current and
expected levels of consumption, the equilibrium rate adjusts in order to clear
financial markets, i.e. the real rate ensures that saving remains zero in equilibrium.

\subsection{Expectations}

How are we supposed to cope with the term $E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}
$\ in the Euler equation from system (\ref{eq:sys1})?\ This question is still
left open in macroeconomics, as there is no perfect specification for the
expectation scheme that portrays sufficiently well the formation process of
expectations of agents over time.

Back in the 60s, macroeconomic analysis was relying on large-scale models
based on old Keynesian economics.\ These models were 'backward looking', in a
sense that they did not feature any type of expectation in their modeling
structure.\ Some purely forward looking dynamics were simply omitted, which
questioned their empirical relevance for policy analysis as argued by
\cite{lucas1976econometric} in his famous critique.

Which forward looking dynamics are we talking about? Let us think about some
simple examples in macroeconomics:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A\ first example is about \textbf{price setting}.\ If the central bank
is accommodative today, a worker may expect that inflation will rise in the
future, so the worker asks today for an increase of her wage to compensate
future inflation (which in turn self-fulfills the increase in inflation now).
These considerations leaded to a New reinterpretation of the Keynesian
Phillips curve.

\item Another example about\textbf{ portfolio allocation in open
economies}.\ Suppose that a central bank in the domestic country rises its
interest rate, following this change, investing in the domestic country is
more profitable, so you may expect that capital will flew to the domestic
country, so you expect that your domestic currency will rise in the future. As
a result, the domestic currency immediately rises to an increase in the
domestic real rate prior the inflow of foreign capitals. This narrative
describes the uncovered interest rate parity condition. These two examples are
simply not incorporated in purely backward models.
\end{enumerate}

Back to our problem of having an expectation scheme, Rational Expectations
(RE) hypothesis has been the most popular tool to solve current macroeconomic
model. RE\ provides a quick, simple and agnostic way of modelling expectations
in macroeconomic models.\ In addition, RE are typically 'easy' to compute, as
you even get closed-form expressions. The concept of rational expectations is
pioneered by \cite{muth1961rational}, an agent having rational expectation
\textbf{use all relevant information} in forming expectations of economic
variables. In our toy model, what is the relevant information for
$E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}  $? Another implicit facet of RE lies in
assumption that agents also have perfect knowledge of the model's
equation.\ So if our agent knows the endowment in \autoref{eq: exo cons}, by
iterating forward this equation we get:%

\begin{align*}
E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}   &  =E_{t}\left\{  1-\rho+\rho y_{t}%
+\varepsilon_{t+1}\right\} \\
&  =E_{t}\left\{  1-\rho+\rho y_{t}\right\} \\
&  =1-\rho+\rho y_{t},
\end{align*}


So as you can see, we are able to transform $E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}
$\ into a system of current and backward variables. Implicitly, you may have
noticed that we imposed $E_{t}\left\{  \varepsilon_{t+1}\right\}  =0$.\ This
is because the agent ignores the future realizations of the shock, but know
its distribution.\ Since on average, $E(\varepsilon_{t})=0$, then it is
rational to expect that -- on average -- the shocks are 0. So agent's
expectations may be wrong, but \textbf{are correct on average} over time.

So, forecast errors are non-zero:%
\[
E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}  -y_{t+1}\neq0\text{ for }t>0
\]
But converge to zero asymptotically:%
\[
E\left(  E_{t}\left\{  y_{t+1}\right\}  \overset{}{-}y_{t+1}\right)
_{t=0}^{+\infty}=0\text{ }%
\]


Back to our two-equation system in \autoref{eq:sys1}, under rational
expectations, the model becomes:%
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
y_{t}=1-\rho+\rho y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\\
r_{t}=\frac{\left(  1-\rho+\rho y_{t}\right)  }{\beta y_{t}}%
\end{array}
\right.  .\label{eq:sys2}%
\end{equation}


Given this system, we are able to simulate our model because as long as we
know the value of output, we can obtain the value of the real interest rate.%

\section{Approximation methods}

In the two-dimensional model considered, it is possible to simulate the
(fully) non-linear model without approximating the equations. However, if the
model gets more complicated (in particular with state variables), it's not
possible to get a closed-form expression of the non-linear model.\ Therefore,
approximation methods are applied to the model's equation to obtain a
closed-form expression of a linear transition equation.

\subsection{The problem}

Our 2 equation model is a non-linear system with rational expectations. In
compact notation, the system can be represented as follows:%
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[  f_{\theta}\left(  x_{t+1},x_{t},x_{t-1},\epsilon
_{t}\right)  \right]  =0 \label{eq non linear system}%
\end{equation}
where:

\begin{itemize}
\item $x$ is the vector collecting $n$ endogenous variables;

\item $\epsilon$ is the vector collecting the $p$ exogenous stochastic shocks;

\item $\theta$ is the vector collecting the deep parameters of the model;

\item $f_{\theta}(.)$ is the equations of the model;
\end{itemize}

The solution to a model like this is normally a set of functions relating
controls to states such that our 2\ equations are satisfied at all times (i.e.
in t+1, t and t-1).The solution of the system $x_{t}$ takes the form a
decision rule:
\begin{equation}
x_{t}=g\left(  x_{t-1},\epsilon_{t}\right)  , \label{eq: non linear DR}%
\end{equation}
where function $g(.)$ is unknown, but theoretically can be computed from
\autoref{eq non linear system}. Let us first rewrite the system in
\autoref{eq non linear system} by injecting the model's solution in
\autoref{eq: non linear DR}. Recall, that under rational expectations,
$E_{t}\{x_{t+1}\}=g\left(  x_{t},\epsilon_{t+1}\right)  $ where $E\left(
\epsilon_{t+1}\right)  =0_{p}$.\ To get rid off of the expectation term in the
previous expression, we obtain:%
\begin{equation}
f_{\theta}\left(  \text{ }g\left(  x_{t},0_{p}\right)  ,\text{ }x_{t},\text{
}y_{t-1},\text{ }\epsilon_{t}\right)  =0.
\end{equation}
Iterating again to express our system as a function of $x_{t-1}$:
\begin{equation}
f_{\theta}\left(  \text{ }g\left(  g\left(  x_{t-1},\epsilon_{t}\right)
,0_{p}\right)  ,\text{ }g\left(  x_{t-1},\epsilon_{t}\right)  ,\text{ }%
x_{t-1},\text{ }\epsilon_{t}\right)  =0 \label{eq: non linear system func}%
\end{equation}
Therefore, the true solution of the system \autoref{eq non linear system} is a
function $g(.)$ that satisfies \autoref{eq: non linear system func}. The
problem is that, in case of larger models, there is an infinite number of
values of $y_{t-1}$ and $\epsilon_{t}$ and an infinite number of values of
$y_{t}$ to find. Hence, except for few exceptions, the usual practice is to
approximate $g(.)$.

Note that in our two-dimensional model of consumption-saving, we are able to
get a closed-form expression $g(.)$ but in more complicated models, it's
impossible to obtain the true value of $g(.)$. The next subsection aims at
explaining how we can approximate $g(.)$.

The closed-form expression of the policy rule $x_{t}=g\left(  x_{t-1}%
,\epsilon_{t}\right)  $ is given by:%
\begin{align}
y_{t}  &  =1-\rho+\rho y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\\
r_{t}  &  =\frac{\rho}{\beta}+\frac{\beta^{-1}\left(  1-\rho\right)  }%
{1-\rho+\rho y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}}.
\end{align}


\subsection{Linearization Method}

The standard way to bound the problem is to approximate the function $g\left(
.\right)  $ by a log-linear function around the steady state. There are many
different solution methods (e.g. Schur decomposition, undetermined
coefficients,\ etc) to reach the linearized version of $g\left(  .\right)  .$
Under particular conditions defined by \cite{blanchard1980solution}, our
linear rational expectations model has a stable and convergent intertemporal equilibrium.

More precisely, we usually take the following steps to solve a model:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Find the steady state of the model;

\item (log-)Linearize around the steady state;

\item Write the Dynare file.
\end{enumerate}

For any function $f(x_{t})$ with corresponding steady state $f(\bar{x})$, a
Taylor expansion up to first order is given by:%
\begin{equation}
f(x_{t})\simeq f(\bar{x})+f^{\prime}(\bar{x})\left(  x_{t}-\bar{x}\right)
\end{equation}
Expressed in terms of deviation from the steady state, $f(x_{t})-f(\bar{x})$,
the expansion can be rewritten in the following way:%
\begin{align*}
f(x_{t})-f(\bar{x})  &  \simeq f^{\prime}(\bar{x})\left(  x_{t}-\bar{x}\right)
\\
&  \simeq f^{\prime}(\bar{x})\bar{x}\frac{\left(  x_{t}-\bar{x}\right)  }%
{\bar{x}}\\
&  \simeq f^{\prime}(\bar{x})\bar{x}\log(\frac{x_{t}}{\bar{x}})\\
&  \simeq f^{\prime}(\bar{x})\bar{x}\hat{x}_{t}%
\end{align*}
where $\hat{x}_{t}=\log(x_{t}/\bar{x})$ is the percentage variations of
$x_{t}$ around its steady state.

For a two-variable function, the approximation reads as:%
\begin{equation}
f(x_{t},y_{t})-f(\bar{x},\bar{y})\simeq f_{x}^{\prime}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\bar
{x}\hat{x}_{t}+f_{y}^{\prime}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\bar{y}\hat{y}_{t}%
\end{equation}
We can them summarize these general approximations by some tricks that we can
use to log-linearize quickly a model:

\begin{center}%
\begin{tabular}
[c]{ll}\hline\hline
\ \ \ \ \textbf{Level variable \ \ \ } & \textbf{Log-linearized variable}%
\\\hline
\multicolumn{1}{c}{$x_{t}$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$\bar{x}\hat{x}_{t}$}\\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{$x_{t}y_{t}$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$\bar{x}\bar{y}(\hat
{x}_{t}+\hat{y}_{t})$}\\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{$\frac{x_{t}}{y_{t}}$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$\frac{\bar{x}%
}{\bar{y}}(\hat{x}_{t}-\hat{y}_{t})$}\\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{$x_{t}^{a}$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$\bar{x}^{a}a\hat{x}_{t}%
$}\\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{$\log(x_{t})$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$\hat{x}_{t}$}\\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{$\exp(x_{t})$} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{$\exp(\bar{x})\bar
{x}\hat{x}_{t}$}\\\hline
\end{tabular}

\end{center}

\subsection{Linearization application}

Let us now linearize our system of equations:%

\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
y_{t}=1-\rho+\rho y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\\
r_{t}=\frac{\left(  1-\rho+\rho y_{t}\right)  }{\beta y_{t}}%
\end{array}
\right.  .
\end{equation}


\textsf{Steady state.\ }A preliminary step before performing the approximation
to our model's equations is to characterize the steady state of the model
(i.e. the fixed point of the approximation).\ Following the linearization,
each variable is expressed in percentage deviation from the steady state. To
obtain the steady state, one just needs to take off the time subscript on
endogenous variables:%
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
\bar{y}=1-\rho+\rho\bar{y}\\
\bar{r}=\frac{\left(  1-\rho+\rho\bar{y}\right)  }{\beta\bar{y}}%
\end{array}
\right.  .
\end{equation}
Note that the following system in the (deterministic) steady state reads as
follows:%
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
\bar{y}=1\\
\bar{r}=\frac{1}{\beta}%
\end{array}
\right.  .
\end{equation}


\textsf{Linearizing the equations. }Next, we must linearize the equations of
the model.\ We apply here:\textsf{\ }%

\[
\underset{\bar{y}\tilde{y}_{t}}{\underbrace{y_{t}}}=\underset{0}%
{\underbrace{1-\rho}}+\underset{\rho\bar{y}\tilde{y}_{t-1}}{\underbrace{\rho
y_{t-1}}}+\underset{\varepsilon_{t}}{\underbrace{\varepsilon_{t}}}%
\]
Note that since $\bar{y}=1$, then equation boils down to:%
\[
\hat{y}_{t}\simeq\rho\hat{y}_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}%
\]
Some remarks on the linearization.\ You may struggle to log-linearize, but
with learning-by-doing, you will get used to it. After some time of practice,
linearizing equations get very quickly. Note also that the stochastic shock is
not affected by the linearization process%

\begin{align*}
r_{t}  &  =\frac{\left(  1-\rho+\rho y_{t}\right)  }{\beta y_{t}}\\
r_{t}  &  =\frac{\left(  1-\rho\right)  }{\beta}\frac{1}{y_{t}}+\frac{\rho
}{\beta}\\
\bar{r}\hat{r}_{t}  &  \simeq-\frac{1-\rho}{\beta\bar{y}}\hat{y}_{t}\\
\hat{r}_{t}  &  \simeq-\left(  1-\rho\right)  \hat{y}_{t}%
\end{align*}


So the linerarized system reads as:%
\begin{align*}
\hat{y}_{t}  &  \simeq\rho\hat{y}_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\\
\hat{r}_{t}  &  \simeq-\left(  1-\rho\right)  \hat{y}_{t}%
\end{align*}
And we are looking for a solution of the form for $\hat{x}_{t}=[\hat{y}%
_{t},\hat{r}_{t}]$:%
\[
\hat{x}_{t}=P\hat{x}_{t-1}+Q\epsilon_{t}%
\]
While equation for $\hat{y}_{t}$\ is recursive, the other in $\hat{r}_{t}$ is
not. We simply inject $\hat{y}_{t}$ in $\hat{r}_{t}$\ to force recursion:
$\hat{r}_{t}\simeq-\left(  1-\rho\right)  \hat{y}_{t}=-\left(  1-\rho\right)
\rho\hat{y}_{t-1}-\left(  1-\rho\right)  \varepsilon_{t}.$The solution of the
system reads as:%
\[
\underset{\hat{x}_{t}}{\underbrace{\left[
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
\hat{y}_{t}\\
\hat{r}_{t}%
\end{array}
\right]  }}=\underset{P}{\underbrace{\left[
\begin{array}
[c]{cc}%
\rho & 0\\
-\left(  1-\rho\right)  \rho & 0
\end{array}
\right]  }}\underset{\hat{x}_{t-1}}{\underbrace{\left[
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
\hat{y}_{t-1}\\
\hat{r}_{t-1}%
\end{array}
\right]  }}+\underset{Q}{\underbrace{\left[
\begin{array}
[c]{c}%
1\\
-\left(  1-\rho\right)
\end{array}
\right]  }}\varepsilon_{t}%
\]
As long as we know vector $\varepsilon_{t}$, matrices $P$\ and $Q$, and
initial conditions $\hat{y}_{0}$\ and $\hat{r}_{0}$, we are able to
characterize the path of $\{\hat{y}_{t},\hat{r}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}$.%
